## Why this lesson matters

Plan and publish a simple personal website that explains who you are, proves what you can do through projects, and gives visitors a clear way to contact you.

The goal is not to memorize vocabulary. By the end of the lesson, you should be able to use the ideas in a realistic situation, explain the reason for your choices, and check whether the result actually works for the intended person or task.

## Learning objectives

- Explain Portfolio in your own words.
- Apply Information architecture to a realistic classroom or community example.
- Connect Portfolio with Call to action when making a decision.
- Complete the practice task and reflect on one improvement.

## Core ideas

### 1. Portfolio

A curated collection of work that demonstrates skills through evidence, context, process, and results rather than claims alone.

**In practice:** Look for this idea while you complete the lesson task. Pause before each major step and explain how Portfolio changes what you choose, create, or check.

### 2. Information architecture

The organization and labeling of content so visitors can quickly understand where they are and where to find what they need.

**In practice:** Look for this idea while you complete the lesson task. Pause before each major step and explain how Information architecture changes what you choose, create, or check.

### 3. Call to action

A specific next step—such as contact, download, or view project—that is clearly visible and easy for a visitor to complete.

**In practice:** Look for this idea while you complete the lesson task. Pause before each major step and explain how Call to action changes what you choose, create, or check.

## How the ideas connect

Start with **Portfolio** to understand the foundation of the lesson. Use **Information architecture** to turn that understanding into an action. Then apply **Call to action** to check the quality, safety, or usefulness of the result. The three ideas are strongest when you can explain their relationship rather than treating them as separate definitions.

## Guided walkthrough

1. **Name the goal.** In one sentence, write what you are trying to understand, create, or improve.
2. **Make a prediction.** Before touching a device, use Portfolio and Information architecture to predict what a strong result should look like.
3. **Complete the task.** Sketch a one-page portfolio on paper with About, Skills, Projects, and Contact sections. For one project, write the problem, your contribution, and the result.
4. **Check the outcome.** Use Call to action to inspect the result. Ask what worked, what did not, and what evidence supports your judgment.
5. **Explain and revise.** Tell a partner what you changed and why. Make one small improvement, then compare the new result with the first one.

## Worked classroom scenario

Imagine two learners sharing one device. The first learner is the **driver** and performs the steps; the second is the **navigator** and reads the goal, predicts the next step, and checks the result. Halfway through the task, switch roles. Both learners should be able to explain how Portfolio, Information architecture, and Call to action appeared in the work.

If no device is available, complete the same reasoning on paper: sketch the screen or result, label each decision, and describe what you would test when a device becomes available.

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Rushing into the tool:** Write the goal and prediction first so every click or step has a reason.
- **Copying without understanding:** After each major step, explain it in your own words to a partner.
- **Accepting the first result:** Compare the outcome with the goal and make at least one deliberate improvement.
- **Letting one person control a shared device:** Rotate driver and navigator roles so both learners think and practice.

## Independent practice

Sketch a one-page portfolio on paper with About, Skills, Projects, and Contact sections. For one project, write the problem, your contribution, and the result.

For an extra challenge, adapt the task for a different audience or community need. Write two sentences explaining what changed and which lesson idea guided your decision.

## Check your understanding

1. How would you explain Portfolio to someone new to the topic?
2. What is one realistic example of Information architecture outside this classroom?
3. When might Call to action prevent a weak, unsafe, or confusing result?
4. How are Portfolio and Information architecture connected?
5. What evidence would convince you that your practice result works?
6. If you repeated the activity tomorrow, what would you improve first and why?

## Key takeaway

Plan and publish a simple personal website that explains who you are, proves what you can do through projects, and gives visitors a clear way to contact you.

You are ready to move on when you can explain the three core ideas, complete the practice without copying, and describe one improvement using evidence from your result.
